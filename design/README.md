# Context

## Instructor

**Jerid C. Francom** — Associate Professor of Spanish and Linguistics, Department of Romance Languages, Wake Forest University (2009–present). Former Associate Chair of Spanish & Italian and Program Director of the Interdisciplinary Linguistics Minor.

My work connects language research, language teaching, and computational methods. I study how Spanish varies across communities and contexts, using corpora, experimental evidence, and statistical programming in R. My recent book, *An Introduction to Quantitative Text Analysis for Linguistics: Reproducible Research Using R* (Routledge, 2025), captures that methodological stance, and my current projects extend it into human-centered LLM workflows for learning and collaboration.

### Core Areas

- **Spanish syntax** – wh-movement, word order, the left periphery, dislocation.
- **Language variation** – cross-dialect Spanish (Argentina, Mexico, Spain), dialectology, pronunciation.
- **Corpus & computational methods** – comparable corpora, finite-state tools, POS tagging, NLP for Spanish.
- **Psycholinguistics** – experimental syntax, lexical access, exposure effects.

### Selected Work

- "Wh-movement: Interrogatives, Exclamatives and Relatives," *The Handbook of Hispanic Linguistics* (Wiley-Blackwell).
- "Corpus Studies of Syntax," *The Cambridge Handbook of Experimental Syntax* (CUP).
- *ACTIV-ES*: a comparable, cross-dialect corpus of everyday Spanish (with Hulden & Ussishkin).
- *An Introduction to Quantitative Text Analysis for Linguistics* (Routledge, 2025).

### Why This Matters for This Seminar

- **Standing in the field.** I contributed a chapter to *The Handbook of Hispanic Linguistics* — the field's standard survey — so this seminar is taught from the inside of the discipline.
- **Syntax as the spine.** Real depth in Spanish syntactic structure (wh-movement, word order, information structure), the traditional heart of Hispanic linguistics.
- **Interdisciplinary range.** Bridges the formal/syntactic tradition with corpus, experimental, and computational methods — the direction the field is moving.

*Framing line:* A seminar in Hispanic linguistics taught from the inside of the field — grounded in Spanish syntax and variation, and carried forward with corpus, experimental, and computational methods.

# Proposal

> STATUS: Early-stage brainstorm, not a finished proposal. The content below is a set of *starting questions and candidate directions* to be fed into a formal backwards-design process (goals → evidence → learning activities → sequence). Do not read the arc, readings, or assessments as decided. They are placeholders to argue with.

**Working title (tentative):** Spanish in Typological Perspective: Universals, Variation, and Contact

**One-line thesis:** *Do Spanish's varieties differ typologically, or only in surface features?* The course uses this question as its engine – taught at a conceptual level, with selective deeper dives, and data central but pre-processed.

## Positioning (why this shape)

- **Center of gravity:** typology as a *lens* on Spanish variation – not typology as a survey of the world's languages. Each unit closes the loop back on Spanish.
- **Level:** conceptual spine + a small number of curated deeper dives (open – see below).
- **Methods:** data-driven throughout; students *interpret* real distributions rather than do intensive programming. Tooling (e.g., Shiny apps over pre-processed corpora) to be designed later.
- **Thesis stance:** moderate, with a critical eye on the evidence and structured open dialogue about "what counts as a distinct type vs. a dialect." No instructor-mandated verdict; students weigh evidence.
- **Strategic role:** intended as an **anchor** for a restructured linguistics track/concentration – so it is built to recruit students in early (placeable right after SPA 320) and to point visibly toward next steps.

## Part I – The Typological Toolkit (foundations, before Spanish)

> Goal: give students the *conceptual and methodological* footing to *do* typology, not just read about it. World-language-centered; the Spanish arc (Part II) then interrogates Spanish with this lens. Keep it lean – teach the method **through one worked micro-example** (a small WALS/Grambank feature), not as an abstract mini-methods course.

### Module 0 – What typology is, how it's done, why (toolkit)

- **What it is / isn't:** comparison of structures across languages to find patterns → contrast with *genetic* classification (Spanish is Ibero-Romance regardless of typology) and with grammar-writing (describing one language). Key habit to unlearn early: **types are continua, not boxes** – no language "is" SOV; it is *more* SOV than not.
- **How it's done** (the three problems that constrain every conclusion):
  - the **sampling problem** – which languages, how many, and are they *independent* data points or shared inheritance / areal contact?
  - the **comparability problem** – what counts as "the same" feature across unrelated languages (the *tertium comparationis*)?
  - **correlation ≠ causation** – areal vs. genealogical vs. functional explanations.
- **Markedness & universals:** Greenberg's **implicational** logic (if X then Y: trial → dual → singular/plural); **statistical vs. absolute** universals; the *why* – frequency, processing, iconicity, economy. Markedness = rarer / later-acquired / first-lost / less-frequent → this one thread is the bridge to Unit B (subjunctive).
- **Design features** (Greenberg's inventory order: phonology → morphology → syntax) as the syllabus spine.
- **Method coherence:** this module is the *one* place to teach what the typological databases actually did (coding, normalization) – which is what licenses the "pre-processed data, no coding" design later, and resolves the black-box concern at its root. Ties to the instructor's *Quantitative Text Analysis* framing.

### Module 1 – Spanish on the typological map (positioning)

- **Defined baseline – "pan-Hispanic":** a technical object = the **cross-varietal common core**, the features shared across the major Spanish varieties synchronically (roughly, the mutually shared normative grammar). NOT a prestige/"correct" variety; variation is treated throughout as *systematic deviation from this baseline*. Defining it is what keeps the course from implying one "real" Spanish.
- **Two markedness scales (state this explicitly):**
  - **Typological markedness** (Part I): Spanish-vs-the-world – how common/rare a feature is across languages.
  - **Internal / areal markedness** (Part II): variety-vs-pan-Hispanic – whether *some* Spanish varieties deviate from the common core.
  Same word, two scales; keep them named so students don't conflate "globally unusual" with "dialectally deviant."
- Score pan-Hispanic against the world's languages in both directions (the symmetry *is* the critical-eye lesson):
  - **Relatively unmarked (a common type):** nominative-accusative alignment; SVO among the two most frequent orders; 5-vowel system /i e a o u/ (modal inventory size); no tone; singular/plural number; a *two*-gender system (most common where gender exists); perfective/imperfective aspect.
  - **Typologically marked (Spanish's surprises):** **null subjects** ("*como*" without "*yo*" – most languages don't allow the drop or don't require the agreement); **subjunctive mood** (most languages have none); **dual copula** *ser/estar*; clitic pronoun + object-marking system; mixed noun-adjective order; neuter residue (*lo*).
- **Setup for Part II:** once students hold "Spanish's marked profile = null-subject + subjunctive + clitics," the pro-drop cline (A) and mood erosion (B) re-frame as one question – *what happens to a language's marked features under variation and contact?* – giving the whole course a single thread.

## Part II – The Spanish arc (three units; candidate, not final)

Repeating unit template – the anti-"two-halves" mechanism. Each unit re-invokes the Part I toolkit (a ~30-second recall of the sampling/comparability problems before the data):

> one typological parameter → its cross-linguistic range → pan-Hispanic baseline → Spanish's varieties placed on it → students analyze real (pre-processed) variation data.

**Convergence-to-universal as a recurring question (threaded through A–C):**

- The cross-unit empirical question: when a variety is *internally* marked (deviates from pan-Hispanic), is that deviation **typologically away from or toward** the world's most common patterns? A "deviant" variety can move *toward* typological normality – e.g., contact/attrition varieties increasing overt subject pronouns push Spanish toward the cross-linguistically dominant non-null-subject pattern, even though pan-Hispanic pro-drop is itself the typologically marked state.
- Treat this as a **tendency to test, not a law** (honest counter-forces):
  - **Selection bias** – we notice marked features eroding toward common types, but miss varieties *gaining* rare/marked ones.
  - **Contact pull** – substrate typology (e.g., Quechua-influenced Andean features) can move a variety toward an *areal*, not globally-common, profile.
- This is Part II's deeper engine: it reframes each unit from "new type vs. frequency shift?" to "*which direction* does contact/attrition pull a variety – toward or away from typological universals?"

### Unit A – Word order & subject expression  *(candidate SHOWCASE 1: deep)*

- Typological lens: subject-expression / pro-drop typology; word-order and information-structure universals.
- Spanish showcase: the **pro-drop cline** – Caribbean (high overt subjects) → Peninsular → Andean (strong retention).
- Fits instructor strengths (syntax, left periphery) and the ACTIV-ES / cross-dialect corpus agenda.
- Starting questions: Can a dialect change its typological profile, or only the *frequency* of a form? What evidence would settle it?

### Unit B – Mood & the subjunctive  *(candidate KEYSTONE: bridges levels)*

- Typological lens: **markedness of irrealis** – subjunctive is low-frequency, semantically "light," allomorph-rich; most of the world's languages have no subjunctive at all.
- Spanish showcase: **contrast the *outcomes*, not the theory** –
  - Equatoguinean Spanish: near-total indicative/subjunctive collapse (Lipski).
  - Los Angeles heritage Spanish: *gradual loss of mood distinctions* (Silva-Corvalán 1994).
  - Monolingual cross-dialect variation (Gal 2019) as the reality check.
- Design caution: teach the **contested diagnosis** (loss vs. form-function renegotiation) as the model of evidence-weighting; do not present "subjunctive loss" as settled fact.
- Deliberately *not* a deep generative-mood unit – runs on data + typology to keep the "not-an-expert" burden low.
- Starting questions: If mood marking is typologically marked, does it erode first under contact? Where do experts actually disagree, and why?

### Unit C – Contact typology  *(candidate SHOWCASE 2: deep / capstone)*

- Typological lens: typological restructuring / substrate influence / creolization.
- Spanish showcase: Quechua → Andean Spanish, Guaraní → Paraguayan Spanish, US Spanish, bozal/Pichi; **synthesizes** mood + vos/vosotros + clitics in one frame.
- Threads SLO 3B "historically marginalized populations" through the *formal* course.
- Starting questions: Can language contact shift a variety's typological profile, and how is that different from ordinary change?

### Conceptual connective tissue: language vs. dialect

- Running frame (Abstand/Ausbau; "a language is a dialect with an army and navy"). Low technical load, high payoff for SLO 3B and language ideology; frames every variety above.
- Bake in **decision criteria** per unit (what would count as evidence for "different type" vs. "different frequency") so open dialogue resolves toward method, not vibes.

## Method design (candidate)

- **Tier 1 (everyone):** explore a pre-built app/visualization – pick a variable, see the dialect distribution, interpret. No code.
- **Tier 2 (opt-in):** guided fill-in-the-blank notebook.
- **Tier 3 (honors/strong):** free analysis.
- **Black-box mitigation (decided: build it in):** one "how the sausage is made" unit + one deliberately *messy* dataset so students see what preprocessing decided for them. Best anchor: the **leveling/regularization** case (*hablaste → hablastes*) – raw corpus → normalized → counts *is* the pipeline.

## Candidate assessments / evidence (stub – do not flesh out yet)

> Deferred to the formal backwards-design pass (goals → evidence → activities). These are placeholders, not designs.

- **Part I:** a small check that students can *do* the typological reasoning (apply the sampling/comparability problems to one worked feature), not just recite definitions – *how* to assess this is a backwards-design decision.
- **Part II:** evidence that students can distinguish the **two markedness scales** (typological vs. internal) and reason about a feature's **convergence/divergence direction** – tied to the keystone/contact units.

## Known gaps / open decisions (to resolve via backwards design)

- **Front-loaded Part I (toolkit + positioning) before the Spanish arc** (decided): students build the conceptual/methodological footing first, then interrogate Spanish with it.
- **Which 2–3 units get the formal/deep treatment** (working assumption: A + C deep, B keystone) – must stay few or depth collapses.
- **Part I time budget** – keep it lean or it becomes a mini-methods course; teach method through one worked micro-example, not abstraction.
- **Reconcile the two markedness stories** – Module 1 frames marked = rare = erodes; Part II reframes the *eroded* outcome as typologically *normal* (pan-Hispanic is the marked state holding it back). Present these as the same phenomenon read on two scales, not a contradiction – a real design decision for the formal doc.
- **Textbook/reading sourcing** – no ready Hispanic-linguistics-typology hybrid; readings are custom.
- **OPE / SLO 1C** – theoretical/comparative ≠ Advanced-Low speaking; a Spanish-oral assessment must be built in, not assumed.
- **Not a 370 replacement** – typology is synchronic/comparative; diachrony arrives only via the leveling/grammaticalization mechanism modules. Flag for the concentration/sequence planning.
- **Cross-listing** – decide relationship with the LIN department (comparative syntax, typology) vs. keeping it a Spanish-major course.
- **Convergence framing** – the Part II "toward/away from typological universals" thread partly *reverses* the Part I premise (Spanish's *retention* of null-subjunctive is the marked global state, so erosion = convergence to the norm). Verify this is consistent across units rather than a subjunctive-only quirk, and keep it a question-to-test, not a thesis-to-defend.
