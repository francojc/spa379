## Stage 1, desired results

### Transfer goal

> By the end of the course — and as an enduring habit — students will be able, **independently and in a new situation**, to act as a critical linguist about Spanish: observe how Spanish actually varies, explain the variation with a testable linguistic account rather than a prescriptive verdict, interrogate the evidence and the categories used to describe it (what counts as "a language," "the norm," "a type," "correct"), and reflect on their own position within that variation — recognizing their own variety as *data*, not the neutral default.

This is the disposition. Everything below feeds it.

**Medium qualifier (Spanish-medium, decided).** The disposition is exercised *in Spanish*: students reason, question, and defend in the L2. "Act as a critical linguist" therefore also means **argue about Spanish variation in Spanish** — which is precisely what makes this course a legitimate site for SLO 1.1 and 1.3, not just SLO 3.2. Stage 2 evidence must be oral/written **in Spanish**.

### Enduring understandings

*(the "big ideas" that outlast the course; each maps to a transfer move)* <!-- TODO: define 'transfer move' -->


| # | Understanding | Transfer move it enables |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **U1** | Linguistic categories (**language, dialect, type, norm, correctness**) are analytical decisions made against criteria — not neutral facts discovered in the data. "A language is a dialect with an army and navy." | Interrogate the categories, not just the data |
| **U2** | Language variation is **systematic and rule-governed** — structured deviation from a baseline, not random error. No variety is "broken Spanish." | Explain with rule/system, not deficit |
| **U3** | Comparison requires a **baseline and a scale**. The *pan-Hispanic common core* (internally vs. varieties) and the *typological profile* (globally vs. the world's languages) are **two distinct measures** that can point in opposite directions. | Use the two markedness scales without conflating them |
| **U4** | Typological **universals are statistical tendencies on continua, not boxes**. No language "is" SOV; it is *more* SOV than not. Markedness = rarer / later-acquired / first-lost / less frequent. | Read frequency/implicational evidence (Greenberg logic) |
| **U5** | All typological comparison is **constrained by method**: the sampling problem, the comparability problem (*tertium comparationis*), and areal vs. genealogical vs. functional confounds (correlation ≠ causation). Data never "just speaks." | Weigh method and evidence quality |
| **U6** | Under variation and contact, a marked feature can be **renegotiated**, and the *outcome* can move a variety **toward or away from** typological commonality. Erosion of a marked structure can = convergence to the global norm. (E.g., rising overt-subject frequency in some contact/heritage varieties moves Spanish toward the dominant non-null-subject pattern.) | Predict/test the *direction* of change |
| **U7** | **Linguistic claims are contested and evidence-weighed**, not settled facts delivered. Experts disagree (e.g., subjunctive "loss" vs. form–function renegotiation) and the disagreement itself is the content. | Defend an interpretation, acknowledge counter-evidence |
| **U8** | The observer is a **data point**. One's own variety carries assumptions; metalinguistic judgment ("sounds right/wrong") is itself an object of analysis. | Suspend judgment + self-reflect |


**The spine.** U3 + U6 are the conceptual core — the *two-markedness-scales* distinction resolves the apparent Part I / Part II reversal: retention and erosion are the **same phenomenon read on two scales**, not a contradiction. The README flagged this as a known gap; treat U3/U6 as its resolution. Everything else hangs off this spine.

### Essential questions

**Course-level (open, no single answer — re-asked each unit):**

- ¿Solo hay una forma "correcta" de hablar español? *(Is there only one "correct" Spanish?)*
- ¿Hay una variedad que sea "la norma", o todas son un dato más? *(Is there a variety that counts as "the norm" — or are they all just further data points?)*
- ¿Hasta qué punto las variedades del español difieren tipológicamente — y no solo en la superficie? *(the README's thesis)*
- ¿Qué convierte una diferencia de frecuencia en un cambio de tipo? *(Where does "type" come from — is it in the language or in the analysis?)*

**Unit-level:**

| Unit | Essential question(s) |
| :-- | :-- |
| Part I — Toolkit (M0–M1) | ¿Qué podemos *saber* sobre una lengua comparándola con otras? ¿Qué decisiones toma el analista *antes* de que "hablen los datos"? ¿Cuándo es una estructura "rara"? |
| A — Word order / pro-drop | ¿Puede un dialecto cambiar su *perfil tipológico*, o solo la *frecuencia* de una forma? ¿Qué evidencia lo decidiría? |
| B — Mood / subjunctive | Si la marcación de modo es tipológicamente marcada, ¿se erosiona primero en contacto? ¿Dónde discrepan realmente los expertos, y por qué? |
| C — Contact (synthesis) | ¿Puede el contacto lingüístico cambiar el perfil tipológico de una variedad — y en qué se diferencia de un cambio "normal"? ¿Hacia *o lejos* de los universales nos mueve el contacto? |
| Frame — language vs. dialect | ¿Dónde está la línea entre lengua y dialecto? ¿Quién la traza, con qué criterios, y con qué consecuencias? |


### Knowledge (students will know…)

**Part I — Toolkit**

- Typology vs. genetic (family) classification; comparison vs. grammar-writing.
- The three method constraints: sampling, comparability (*tertium comparationis*), correlation ≠ causation (areal/genealogical/functional).
- Greenberg: implicational universals (trial → dual → singular/plural); statistical vs. absolute universals; markedness diagnostics (rare / late-acquired / first-lost / low-frequency).
- Design features as an ordering principle (phonology → morphology → syntax).
- The **pan-Hispanic baseline** as a technical object (cross-varietal common core; the RAE *Nueva gramática* as a Spanish-language reference for it) — *not* a prestige norm.
- **Two markedness scales** defined and named (typological vs. internal/areal).
- Spanish's typological profile — unmarked (nom-acc alignment, SVO, 5-vowel system, no tone, two-gender, PF/IMP aspect) vs. marked (null subjects, subjunctive, dual copula *ser/estar*, clitics, mixed N-Adj order, neuter *lo*).
- What the typological databases (WALS / Grambank / languatypologies) actually *did* to produce their data (coding, normalization) — this licenses "pre-processed data" and kills the black-box critique.

**Part II — the arc (A deep, B deep, C synthesis)**

- **A:** pro-drop typology; word-order & information-structure universals; the **overt-subject cline** as the showcase. **Data caveat (WARN):** ACTIV-ES = Argentine, Mexican, Peninsular only (TV/film transcriptions) — it supplies the Peninsular and one Latin-American endpoint, but **not** the Caribbean (high-overt) or Andean (strong-retention) ends that *define* the cline. The full cline must draw on published variationist counts (and/or other corpora, e.g., CORDING/OrCOST-type resources) fed into the Tier-1 app, **not ACTIV-ES alone**.
- **B:** irrealis markedness; the *contrasted outcomes* (Equatoguinean collapse / Lipski; LA heritage mood loss / Silva-Corvalán 1994; cross-dialect reality check / Gal 2019); the contested diagnosis (loss vs. renegotiation).
- **C:** restructuring / substrate / creolization; Quechua→Andean, Guaraní→Paraguayan, US Spanish, bozal/Pichi; synthesis of mood + *vos/vosotros* + clitics.
- **Frame:** *Abstand*/*Ausbau*; language ideology; Brown & Gilman T/V (the *vos/vosotros* social layer).

> Note: all knowledge items above are stated in English here for design clarity but are **delivered in Spanish** (terminology glossed) per the language-of-instruction decision. Keep a running *glosario* (supports the broad+supports audience).

## AI literacy strand (D6/D7 — reshaped per review)

Aligned with `documents/2026-07-27-learning-outcomes-ai-strategies-v01.md` and your LLM-workflow agenda. **Not a recurring theme** (an earlier "daily warm-up" framing was rejected in review); placement + depth are a Stage 2 decision.

**The sharpest angle — GenAI-as-aggregate.** A language model is a *statistical aggregate over a (skewed, unrepresentative) training corpus*. That is **the sampling problem (U5) made concrete and device-native on every laptop**: does that aggregate reflect how Spanish is actually used, whole or in part? Students interrogate whether GenAI flattens dialect to a standard norm, erases *vos/ustedes*/register distinctions, answers descriptive questions prescriptively, or over-confidently infers speaker identity. Auditing that is U8/U2 in action and lands on SLO 3.2.2/3.2.3 + 1.1.5 — and it reframes "is the model's Spanish a *data point* or *the norm*?" (the course's own essential question).

**Secondary use — GenAI-as-tooling.** Students *may* use GenAI to help build analysis pipelines / communicate findings (consistent with how the instructor co-develops tooling — D4). Transferable (liberal-arts), **not** a primary outcome, and not separately graded toward SLOs.

**Three distinct AI roles (D7 policy basis for the syllabus):**
1. **AI-as-infrastructure** — the *instructor* builds the site/data/tooling with LLM support (D4).
2. **AI-as-object-of-analysis** — the *discrete* aggregate-audit activity.
3. **AI-as-tooling** — student *optional* use for pipeline-building.

**Guardrail (per the AI-strategy doc).** Any *assessed* use must name the outcome, keep a **non-delegable core** (student interprets the real corpus *before* consulting the model), and use outcome-linked policy language — not "AI allowed/prohibited."
